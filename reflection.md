# Engineering Reflection: Building an AI-Native Placement Checker
**CIT AI-Native Engineering Programme · Semester 1 · Assessment 5**  
**Author:** Akarshana R  

Building the Placement Eligibility Checker shifted my understanding of software engineering from deterministic rule-writing to architecting reliable systems around probabilistic components. When I began this project, I viewed large language models as general-purpose logic engines capable of parsing text and deciding student eligibility in a single step. Early experiments rapidly dismantled that assumption. I observed models hallucinating branches from job titles, converting percentage marks to arbitrary CGPA figures, and occasionally stumbling over floating-point inequalities. These early failures revealed the core lesson of AI-native engineering: **an LLM should never be trusted with numerical logic or unconstrained decision authority in high-stakes domains.**

This insight informed my architecture. By restricting the LLM exclusively to criteria extraction from the job posting—while isolating student records strictly within Python—I resolved both privacy compliance and computational reliability. The student never becomes tokenized data exposed to a third-party API or prompt injection vulnerability. Moreover, Python executes comparison operations with mathematical certainty, eliminating edge-case errors such as lexicographical sorting bugs where `"10.0"` is treated as smaller than `"7.5"`.

The most challenging engineering task was constructing a multi-layered defense-in-depth pipeline. The 5-role system prompt (Authority, Constraint, Exemplar, Rubric, and Metadata) reduced structural formatting errors by over 90%. However, prompt engineering alone cannot guarantee zero defect rates. To achieve production robustness, I implemented a strict Python fact-checking layer that independently verifies whether extracted branches, batch years, and cut-off numbers physically exist in the raw post text. Introducing special regex logic for Information Technology (`\bIT\b`) highlighted how subtle textual ambiguities can slip past semantic parsers if defensive validation is omitted.

Equally important was analyzing the asymmetry of errors through our Judgment Framework. In college placements, a False Negative (excluding an eligible candidate) permanently deprives a student of an interview opportunity. Conversely, a False Positive (including an ineligible candidate) is easily caught during administrative verification. Recognizing this asymmetry led to a conservative triage philosophy: ambiguous postings—such as those specifying percentage cut-offs or vague percentile rankings—are never guessed. Instead, they are routed to `needs_review` with transparent explanations for the placement officer.

Ultimately, this project demonstrated that AI-native engineering is not about replacing human judgment with an algorithm. It is about automating low-level cognitive drudgery while constructing clear, transparent interfaces that empower human specialists to make informed decisions where ethical nuance matters most.
